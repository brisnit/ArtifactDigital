*Why a CMS migration should begin with subtraction, not transportation.*

There's a question I wish every organization asked before starting a CMS migration.

Not *"Which platform should we choose?"* Not *"Should we move to Sitecore, Optimizely, Drupal, Contentful, or Adobe Experience Manager?"* Not even *"How long will the migration take?"*

The question should be much simpler.

**"Does this content still deserve to exist?"**

Because here's what happens in most enterprise CMS migrations. Organizations spend millions of dollars modernizing their technology while quietly moving years of unnecessary content into a brand-new platform.

Thousands of pages. Outdated PDFs. Abandoned campaign microsites. Press releases no one will ever read again. Duplicate product descriptions. Old landing pages. Broken documentation. Legacy navigation structures. Archived promotional content.

Everything gets packed into boxes and carried into a beautiful new house.

The technology changes. The clutter doesn't.

The result is predictable. The new platform launches with the same search problems. The same bloated navigation. The same inconsistent messaging. The same maintenance burden. The same publishing headaches.

Organizations celebrate a successful migration while wondering why the website still feels difficult to use.

It's because they migrated everything. They never questioned whether everything belonged.

The most successful CMS migrations don't begin with transportation. They begin with subtraction.

## A Migration Is the Best Opportunity You'll Ever Have to Simplify

Content has a strange habit of accumulating.

Every campaign creates new pages. Every product launch adds documentation. Every acquisition introduces another content library. Every business unit publishes independently. Every regional office creates localized variations.

Very little ever disappears.

Unlike a physical office, websites don't naturally become crowded enough to force cleanup. Storage is cheap. Publishing is easy. Deleting feels risky. So content quietly expands for years.

Eventually, organizations wake up and discover they have 5,000 pages. Or 20,000. Sometimes 100,000. Nobody planned for that. It simply happened.

A CMS migration is often the first time the organization is forced to inventory everything it owns. That's incredibly valuable. Not because you're preparing content for a new platform. Because you're finally seeing the true size of your digital estate.

Treat that moment as an opportunity — not an inconvenience.

## Every Page Has a Cost

One misconception about digital content is that it's free. Creating it isn't. Maintaining it certainly isn't.

Every page introduces ongoing responsibility. Someone owns it. Someone updates it. Someone reviews it. Someone ensures it's accurate. Someone verifies accessibility. Someone checks legal compliance. Someone confirms branding. Someone fixes broken links. Someone translates it. Someone eventually migrates it again.

Content isn't an asset simply because it exists. It's a liability until it continues creating value.

Organizations often budget carefully for technology. Very few budget honestly for content maintenance.

## More Content Rarely Means Better Experiences

Enterprise organizations frequently assume that comprehensive means helpful. Customers usually disagree.

When someone visits your website, they're not hoping to find 18 nearly identical articles explaining the same topic. They're hoping to find the right answer quickly.

Content overload creates friction. Navigation becomes overwhelming. Search results become cluttered. Duplicate information competes with itself. Outdated advice undermines trust. Customers spend more time evaluating which page is correct instead of solving their problem.

Quantity often becomes the enemy of clarity. The goal isn't publishing more. It's making the right content easier to find.

## Meet ROT: Redundant, Outdated, and Trivial

One of the simplest frameworks for evaluating content comes from an idea that has been around for years: **ROT**. Redundant. Outdated. Trivial.

It's surprisingly effective.

### Redundant

Redundant content answers the same question multiple times.

The same product explanation exists in five locations. The same company history appears across multiple business units. Regional offices duplicate corporate content. Microsites recreate pages already available elsewhere.

Redundancy confuses customers. Which version is correct? Which one is current? Which one should search engines prioritize?

Consolidation almost always improves both user experience and maintainability.

### Outdated

Outdated content quietly damages credibility.

Expired events. Old executive biographies. Products no longer sold. Services that changed years ago. COVID notices from 2021. Press releases from acquisitions that are no longer relevant. Pricing that's inaccurate. Broken screenshots. Old statistics.

Nothing erodes trust faster than information that obviously hasn't been reviewed. Customers begin wondering: "If this page is wrong… what else is?"

### Trivial

Trivial content is perhaps the hardest category. It's technically accurate. It simply doesn't matter.

A campaign page that served its purpose years ago. Minor internal announcements. Thin blog posts written only for SEO. One-paragraph landing pages. Placeholder resources. Duplicate FAQs. Documents no customer has downloaded in years.

Not everything deserves permanent residence. Sometimes content completed its job. It's okay to let it go.

## Start With the Customer, Not the Sitemap

One mistake organizations make is auditing content page by page. That's exhausting.

Instead, begin with customer intent. Ask simple questions. What are customers actually trying to accomplish? Learn about products? Request support? Compare solutions? Apply for jobs? Contact sales? Find technical documentation?

Complete those journeys first. Everything else becomes easier to evaluate.

If content doesn't support a meaningful customer need or business objective, its future becomes much easier to question.

Customer journeys should determine content priorities — not historical publishing habits.

## Content Owners Often Don't Know What They Own

One of the most revealing moments in enterprise migrations happens during stakeholder interviews.

Ask someone: "What content does your team own?" Most people answer confidently. Then show them the actual inventory.

Thousands of forgotten pages suddenly appear. Old campaigns. Archived resources. Duplicated PDFs. Retired product information. Legacy press releases. Microsites nobody remembered.

It's rarely intentional. Organizations simply lose visibility over time. [Ownership becomes ambiguous.](/insights/enterprise-websites-fail-no-one-owns-experience)

Without clear ownership, content slowly becomes permanent. The migration becomes an opportunity to restore accountability.

Every page should have an owner. If nobody owns it… why does it still exist?

## Don't Treat Every Page Equally

Not every piece of content deserves the same effort. A homepage deserves extraordinary attention. An archived webinar from six years ago probably doesn't.

Prioritize strategically. Mission-critical content. Revenue-driving content. Customer support resources. Product information. Regulatory documentation. High-performing educational content.

Everything else earns its place.

Migration projects often waste enormous effort polishing low-value content while mission-critical experiences receive insufficient attention.

Value — not volume — should drive prioritization.

## Analytics Tell an Honest Story

Organizations sometimes become emotionally attached to content. "We worked really hard on that." "Leadership requested it." "It used to perform well."

Fortunately, analytics are remarkably objective.

Look at traffic, search visibility, time on page, conversions, downloads, internal search behavior, support ticket references, and engagement trends.

Some pages consistently prove their value. Others quietly disappear from customer behavior.

[Analytics shouldn't make every decision. But they should absolutely inform them.](/insights/from-business-goals-to-user-outcomes) Content nobody reads deserves extra scrutiny.

## Migrations Are the Perfect Time to Rewrite

Many organizations assume migration means copying. It often shouldn't.

Legacy content reflects yesterday's organization. Old terminology. Outdated messaging. Previous brand voice. Different customer priorities. Products that evolved. Market positioning that changed.

A migration creates an opportunity to rewrite — not simply relocate.

Sometimes reducing ten pages into one exceptional page creates significantly more value than preserving all ten. Quality scales better than quantity.

## Platform Choice Won't Fix Content Strategy

I've seen organizations spend months evaluating enterprise CMS platforms. Sitecore. Optimizely. Drupal. Adobe Experience Manager. Contentful.

Each platform offers impressive capabilities. Flexible components. Personalization. Workflow automation. Localization. Analytics. AI assistance.

Those features matter. But [none of them automatically improve poor content](/insights/your-cms-is-not-your-digital-strategy).

A modern CMS can't compensate for unnecessary pages. It can't eliminate duplication by itself. It can't determine whether outdated information still belongs online.

Technology enables better publishing. It doesn't decide what deserves publishing. That's still an organizational responsibility.

## Content Governance Begins Before Migration

Many organizations postpone governance until after launch. We'll clean things up later. We'll establish workflows next quarter. We'll define ownership once the migration finishes.

That almost never happens. Old habits return. Publishing accelerates. Content begins accumulating again.

[Governance should begin before the first page moves.](/insights/new-cms-cannot-fix-old-operating-model) Who approves new content? Who owns existing content? How often is it reviewed? When should content expire? Who decides deletion? What standards define quality?

Those answers matter far more than migration scripts.

## AI Makes Content Audits Faster — Not Automatic

Artificial intelligence is becoming an incredibly useful partner during migrations. It can identify duplicates. Summarize documents. Recommend consolidations. Flag outdated language. Analyze readability. Suggest metadata. Detect inconsistent terminology.

That's valuable. But [AI cannot determine organizational importance](/insights/ai-transformation-is-organizational-transformation). It doesn't know political context. It doesn't understand strategic priorities. It can't recognize which content builds trust with your customers despite relatively low traffic.

Judgment remains human. AI accelerates the audit. It doesn't replace it.

## Subtraction Creates Better Search

Search quality is directly influenced by content quality.

Imagine searching your own website for "implementation." Fifty-seven nearly identical pages appear. Some outdated. Some duplicated. Some partially relevant.

Which result should the customer trust?

Removing unnecessary content often improves search more dramatically than investing in better search technology.

Less noise creates stronger signals. Customers find answers faster. Search engines understand authority more clearly. Everyone benefits.

## Simplicity Is Easier to Maintain

Every organization eventually asks the same question. "How do we keep the new CMS from becoming cluttered again?"

The answer isn't more technology. It's operational discipline.

Review content regularly. Archive aggressively. Delete confidently. Measure usefulness. Assign ownership. Celebrate simplification.

A website should evolve like a well-maintained garden — not an abandoned storage facility. Healthy ecosystems require pruning. Digital ecosystems are no different.

## What We Believe at Artifact

At Artifact, we don't think of CMS migrations as technology projects. We think of them as organizational editing exercises.

Technology certainly matters. Architecture matters. Component systems matter. Governance matters. But before any of that, organizations should ask a much more fundamental question: "What deserves to survive?"

We've worked with enterprises whose websites contained thousands of pages accumulated over decades. The most successful projects weren't those that migrated everything flawlessly. They were the ones courageous enough to let go.

They recognized that the future didn't require carrying every decision from the past.

Sometimes the best migration strategy is deleting thousands of pages before writing a single line of migration code.

## A New Platform Deserves a Better Foundation

Imagine moving into a new home. Would you pack every broken appliance? Every outdated manual? Every box you've ignored for fifteen years? Every piece of furniture that no longer fits your life?

Probably not. You'd sort. Donate. Recycle. Throw things away. Only then would you move.

Enterprise CMS migrations deserve the same discipline. Because the platform isn't really the transformation. The thinking is.

The organizations that get the greatest return from Sitecore, Optimizely, Drupal, Contentful, or any other enterprise CMS aren't simply the ones that implement the technology well. They're the ones that recognize migration as an opportunity to redefine what their digital experience should become.

Not bigger. Not newer. Better.

The next generation of enterprise websites won't be remembered because they contained more content than their competitors. They'll be remembered because customers found exactly what they needed — without searching through everything they didn't.

That's the real goal of a migration. Not moving content. Creating clarity.

And clarity almost always begins with subtraction.
